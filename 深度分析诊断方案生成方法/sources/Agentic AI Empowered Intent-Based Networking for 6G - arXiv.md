###### Abstract

The transition towards sixth-generation (6G) wireless networks necessitates autonomous orchestration mechanisms capable of translating high-level operational intents into executable network configurations. Existing approaches to Intent-Based Networking (IBN) rely upon either rule-based systems that struggle with linguistic variation or end-to-end neural models that lack interpretability and fail to enforce operational constraints. This paper presents a hierarchical multi-agent framework where Large Language Model (LLM) based agents autonomously decompose natural language intents, consult domain-specific specialists, and synthesise technically feasible network slice configurations through iterative reasoning-action (ReAct) cycles. The proposed architecture employs an orchestrator agent coordinating two specialist agents, i.e., Radio Access Network (RAN) and Core Network agents, via ReAct-style reasoning, grounded in structured network state representations. Experimental evaluation across diverse benchmark scenarios shows that the proposed system outperforms rule-based systems and direct LLM prompting, with architectural principles applicable to Open RAN (O-RAN) deployments.
The results also demonstrate that whilst contemporary LLMs possess general telecommunications knowledge, network automation requires careful prompt engineering to encode context-dependent decision thresholds, advancing autonomous orchestration capabilities for next-generation wireless systems.

Agentic AI, 6G, Multi-Agent Systems, Intent-Based Networking, Network Orchestration

## I Introduction

The ongoing transition towards sixth-generation (6G) wireless networks necessitates fundamental advances in autonomous orchestration capabilities. As articulated by Wang et al. [[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib1 "On the road to 6g: visions, requirements, key technologies, and testbeds")], 6G systems envision ubiquitous intelligence and zero-touch network management that transcend the operational paradigms of prior generations. Central to this vision is Intent-Based Networking (IBN), where network operators specify high-level business objectives in natural language that are automatically translated into device-specific configurations [[16](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib4 "A survey on intent-based networking")]. However, existing IBN approaches rely upon either structured policy languages that impose accessibility barriers for non-technical operators, or rule-based systems that struggle with linguistic variation and fail to generalise beyond explicitly programmed patterns. This semantic gap between natural language intent and technical implementation represents a critical bottleneck for autonomous network management at scale.

Large Language Models (LLMs) offer promising capabilities for bridging this gap through their natural language understanding and generation abilities. Recent surveys have documented LLM applications across telecommunications domains [[39](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib3 "Large language model (LLM) for telecommunications: a comprehensive survey on principles, key techniques, and opportunities")], including network configuration generation [[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib25 "Netconfeval: can LLMs facilitate network configuration?")], fault diagnosis, and telecom-specific language model development [[40](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib24 "Telecomgpt: a framework to build telecom-specific large language models")]. However, LLMs exhibit fundamental limitations that constrain their direct applicability to autonomous network orchestration. As demonstrated by Wu et al. [[35](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib5 "Reasoning or reciting? exploring the capabilities and limitations of language models through counterfactual tasks")], contemporary LLMs excel at pattern recitation rather than genuine reasoning, with performance degrading substantially on counterfactual tasks. More critically, LLMs function as text generation systems lacking autonomous decision-making capabilities. Whilst an LLM can generate syntactically correct configurations, it cannot independently verify feasibility against current network state, coordinate multi-stage provisioning workflows, or adapt dynamically to resource constraints.

Agentic AI systems address above limitations by positioning LLMs as reasoning engines within broader architectures that incorporate planning, tool use, and environmental interaction [[32](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib31 "A survey on large language model based autonomous agents")]. These systems have demonstrated effectiveness across domains including residential energy management [[21](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib6 "Agentic AI home energy management system: a large language model framework for residential load scheduling")], robotic control [[26](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib7 "Towards embodied agentic ai: review and classification of llm-and vlm-driven robot autonomy and interaction")], and software engineering [[24](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib9 "Agentic AI software engineer: programming with trust")]. In telecommunications, Jiang et al. [[14](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib8 "Large language model enhanced multi-agent systems for 6G communications")] proposed multi-agent frameworks for 6G parameter optimisation, demonstrating cooperative agent coordination through natural language. However, existing work has focused primarily on parameter optimisation rather than autonomous multi-step orchestration workflows requiring iterative reasoning and constraint satisfaction across multiple network domains.

This paper presents a hierarchical agentic AI framework for IBN in 6G environments, where multiple LLM agents collaboratively translate natural language operational intents into executable network slice configurations. Inspired by the hierarchical multi-agent framework demonstrated in [[21](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib6 "Agentic AI home energy management system: a large language model framework for residential load scheduling")], we develop a system employing one orchestrator agent and two specialist agents that collaboratively analyse network state, generate provisioning recommendations, and execute configuration decisions. The architecture employs an orchestrator agent coordinating two domain-specific specialists, i.e., Radio Access Network (RAN) and Core Network agents, through ReAct-style reasoning [[37](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib10 "ReAct: synergizing reasoning and acting in language models")], with all agents grounded in structured network state representations. The system operates without predefined workflow templates, relying solely on carefully engineered system prompts to encode domain expertise and operational policies. Experimental validation across diverse benchmark scenarios reveals that the proposed multi-agent architecture outperforms monolithic agents, rule-based systems, and direct LLM prompting approaches. Critically, the evaluation process uncovered systematic prompt-induced biases that manifested as consistent failure patterns rather than random errors, demonstrating that seemingly minor linguistic variations in system prompts can induce measurable behavioural differences with consequential downstream effects. The identification and resolution of these biases through targeted prompt refinement validates prompt engineering as a critical architectural component requiring rigorous validation protocols.

The principal contributions of this work are threefold, as outlined below:

1. 1.

   We establish a hierarchical multi-agent architecture specifically designed for IBN orchestration, demonstrating how domain specialisation and iterative reasoning enable autonomous translation of natural language intents into technically feasible configurations. The architecture addresses fundamental limitations of monolithic LLM approaches by decomposing complex orchestration tasks across specialised agents, each encoding domain-specific expertise through carefully engineered system prompts.
2. 2.

   We introduce a hybrid evaluation framework combining semantic accuracy, i.e., alignment with expert-defined benchmarks and engineering utility, i.e., quantitative assessment of technical optimality to comprehensively assess agentic system performance beyond traditional correctness metrics. This dual-metric approach enables validation that generated configurations not only align with expert intent interpretation but also satisfy operational constraints and optimise resource allocation across multiple quality dimensions.
3. 3.

   We provide empirical evidence that prompt engineering introduces subtle but systematic biases in agent behaviour, demonstrating that seemingly minor linguistic variations in system prompts can induce measurable performance differences. The identification and resolution of a “latency greedy” bias through targeted prompt refinement highlights the necessity for systematic validation and error pattern analysis in production deployments of LLM-based autonomous systems, establishing prompt engineering as a critical architectural component requiring rigorous testing protocols.

## II Related Works

The convergence of IBN paradigms, machine learning advances, and LLM capabilities has created new opportunities for autonomous network orchestration. This section surveys prior work across four interconnected research domains, concluding with identification of the research gap that motivates our hierarchical multi-agent architecture.

### II-A Intent-Based Networking and Network Automation

IBN emerged as a paradigm shift towards policy-driven network management, where high-level business objectives are automatically translated into device-specific configurations. The foundational principles were established through Software-Defined Networking (SDN), which introduced programmable control-data plane separation [[15](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib13 "Software-defined networking: a comprehensive survey")]. Leivadeas and Falkner [[16](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib4 "A survey on intent-based networking")] conducted the definitive comprehensive survey, establishing systematic taxonomy for intent expression, translation, resolution, activation, and assurance. Their work extensively discusses natural language processing-based intent expression and translation of abstract intents into network configurations, identifying the semantic gap between business-level intents and technical policy representations as a principal limitation.

Pang et al. [[23](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib14 "A survey on intent-driven networks")] established foundational intent-driven network architecture emphasising decoupled control logic for automated intent handling, whilst Benzaid and Taleb [[5](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib15 "AI-driven zero touch network and service management in 5G and beyond: challenges and research directions")] positioned AI-driven automation as the key enabler for zero-touch service management framework. However, these approaches inherited fundamental limitations of requiring structured policy languages rather than natural language interfaces. Recent knowledge-graph approaches have attempted to bridge the semantic gap through ontology-based mapping, but demonstrated brittleness when encountering linguistic variations not explicitly encoded in knowledge bases.

The emergence of network slicing in 5G and beyond introduced additional orchestration complexity. Foukas et al. [[11](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib16 "Network slicing in 5G: survey and challenges")] established the seminal end-to-end slicing framework across radio access network, transport, and core domains, identifying orchestration complexity as the primary research challenge. Afolabi et al. [[2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib17 "Network slicing and softwarization: a survey on principles, enabling technologies, and solutions")] provided comprehensive coverage of SDN-based slicing mechanisms, detailing management and orchestration architectures and network slice templates that directly relate to slice configuration output formats. Coronado et al. [[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib18 "Zero touch management: a survey of network automation solutions for 5G and 6G networks")] surveyed zero-touch management solutions, explaining why human-in-the-loop management fails at 6G scale and justifying the necessity for autonomous intent translation mechanisms.

Recent O-RAN-based orchestration frameworks have demonstrated intent-driven automation capabilities. D’Oro et al. [[8](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib41 "OrchestRAN: orchestrating network intelligence in the open RAN")] introduced OrchestRAN, which translates high-level operator control objectives into optimised near-real-time application (xApp) and non-real-time application (rApp) deployments, formulating intent realisation as an NP-hard optimisation problem and achieving sub-second computation times for large-scale networks. Similarly, Bonati et al. [[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib42 "NeutRAN: an open RAN neutral host architecture for zero-touch RAN and spectrum sharing")] proposed NeutRAN, implementing zero-touch automation pipelines that provision complete network slices in approximately 10 seconds based on operator requests. However, both systems operate within structured intent specification frameworks requiring predefined parameters or templates. Whilst these approaches excel at resource optimisation given explicit numerical constraints, they lack the semantic reasoning capabilities necessary to process unstructured natural language expressions containing implicit requirements. Our work addresses this limitation by positioning LLMs as the semantic interpretation layer, enabling genuine natural language interfaces whilst maintaining automated orchestration principles.

### II-B Machine Learning for Network Optimisation

The application of machine learning techniques to network optimisation has evolved from classical analytical methods such as convex optimization to contemporary deep learning approaches. Deep reinforcement learning has emerged as the dominant paradigm for dynamic network management. Luong et al. [[19](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib19 "Applications of deep reinforcement learning in communications and networking: a survey")] conducted the foundational survey covering deep reinforcement learning applications across network layers including dynamic spectrum access, resource allocation, and traffic routing. Zhang et al. [[38](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib20 "Deep learning in mobile and wireless networking: a survey")] provided encyclopedic coverage of deep learning across the protocol stack, from physical layer modulation to resource allocation. These approaches demonstrated performance improvements over manually designed algorithms but faced fundamental limitations for IBN applications.

Hua et al. [[12](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib21 "GAN-powered deep distributional reinforcement learning for resource management in network slicing")] proposed GAN-powered distributional reinforcement learning for demand-aware network slicing, whilst Eisen and Ribeiro [[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib22 "Optimal wireless resource allocation with random edge graph neural networks")] introduced random edge graph neural networks exploiting network topology for scalable resource allocation. However, these methods require numerical state representations that preclude direct processing of natural language intents. Training necessitates extensive simulation or real-world interaction, which is infeasible for safety-critical telecommunications infrastructure. Perhaps most critically for operational deployment, learned policies function as black-box decision systems lacking the interpretability required in regulated environments. Our work addresses these limitations by leveraging LLMs’ natural language understanding capabilities whilst maintaining grounded reasoning through structured network state representations.

### II-C Large Language Models in Communications

The rapid advancement of foundation models has created new opportunities for natural language interfaces to complex technical systems. Zhou et al. [[39](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib3 "Large language model (LLM) for telecommunications: a comprehensive survey on principles, key techniques, and opportunities")] conducted the definitive survey documenting LLM applications in telecommunications, establishing principles for LLM deployment including architecture design, pre-training strategies, and evaluation methodologies. Their comprehensive taxonomy covers generation, classification, optimisation, and prediction tasks, providing foundational context for LLM-enabled telecom applications.

Recent work has explored LLM adaptation specifically for networking domains. Wu et al. [[34](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib23 "Netllm: adapting large language models for networking")] introduced NetLLM, demonstrating the first framework for adapting LLMs to networking tasks with multimodal encoders and efficient fine-tuning, achieving 10 to 40 percent improvements over state-of-the-art methods on diverse tasks. Zou et al. [[40](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib24 "Telecomgpt: a framework to build telecom-specific large language models")] established systematic pipelines for continual pre-training and instruction tuning to build telecom-specific models, whilst Wang et al. [[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib25 "Netconfeval: can LLMs facilitate network configuration?")] provided systematic evaluation of LLM capabilities for network configuration generation, identifying limitations and proposing design principles for LLM-based configuration systems.

Bariah et al. [[3](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib26 "Large generative AI models for telecom: the next big thing?")] articulated the vision of large generative models as enablers of autonomous wireless networks where multi-modal models eliminate task-specific AI requirements. Maatouk et al. [[20](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib27 "Large language models for telecom: forthcoming impact on the industry")] provided practical industry-focused assessment of LLM readiness for anomaly resolution and technical specification comprehension. Mekrache et al. [[22](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib28 "Intent-based management of next-generation networks: an LLM-centric approach")] proposed LLM-centric Intent Life-Cycle management architecture addressing complete intent lifecycle including decomposition, translation, negotiation, activation, and assurance. However, these applications primarily utilised LLMs as auxiliary tools for generating candidate solutions or single-shot prompting for isolated tasks, rather than autonomous orchestration engines employing iterative multi-agent reasoning protocols.

### II-D Agentic AI and Multi-Agent Systems

Agentic AI systems extend beyond passive text generation to encompass autonomous goal-directed behaviour, tool use, and environmental interaction. The ReAct paradigm introduced by Yao et al. [[37](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib10 "ReAct: synergizing reasoning and acting in language models")] demonstrated that interleaving reasoning traces with action execution enables iterative task refinement whilst producing interpretable decision traces. Wei et al. [[33](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib29 "Chain-of-thought prompting elicits reasoning in large language models")] established that intermediate reasoning steps enable complex multi-step reasoning through Chain-of-Thought prompting, providing foundational principles for orchestrator-specialist iterative refinement. Schick et al. [[27](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib30 "Toolformer: language models can teach themselves to use tools")] introduced Toolformer, demonstrating LLMs’ capacity for autonomous tool selection and invocation without sacrificing core language capabilities.

Wang et al. [[32](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib31 "A survey on large language model based autonomous agents")] provided unified architectural framework for LLM-based autonomous agents encompassing profiling, memory, planning, and action modules directly applicable to describing orchestrator and specialist agent architectures. Li et al. [[17](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib32 "Camel: communicative agents for\" mind\" exploration of large language model society")] introduced role-playing and inception prompting mechanisms for autonomous cooperation between LLM agents, discussing challenges including looping behaviours and role drift in multi-agent systems. Xi et al. [[36](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib33 "The rise and potential of large language model based agents: a survey")] surveyed single-agent, multi-agent, and human-agent cooperation scenarios, contextualising hierarchical agent architectures applicable to distributed network management.

However, a critical challenge identified across LLM applications is reliability and systematic bias mitigation. Si et al. [[29](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib34 "Prompting GPT-3 to be reliable")] decomposed reliability into generalizability, social biases, calibration, and factuality, establishing empirically validated prompting methods for mission-critical applications. This work directly addresses prompt engineering challenges identified in our experimental findings, where seemingly minor linguistic variations in system prompts induce measurable behavioural differences that compound across deployment scenarios.

### II-E Research Gap and Positioning

The literature reveals a persistent gap between IBN requirements and current capabilities. Traditional IBN systems require structured policy languages imposing accessibility barriers [[16](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib4 "A survey on intent-based networking"), [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib14 "A survey on intent-driven networks")]. Machine learning approaches for network optimisation demonstrate strong performance on numerical tasks but cannot process natural language intents and lack interpretability [[19](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib19 "Applications of deep reinforcement learning in communications and networking: a survey"), [38](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib20 "Deep learning in mobile and wireless networking: a survey")]. Recent LLM applications in telecommunications have focused primarily on single-shot prompting [[39](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib3 "Large language model (LLM) for telecommunications: a comprehensive survey on principles, key techniques, and opportunities"), [34](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib23 "Netllm: adapting large language models for networking")] or auxiliary configuration assistance [[30](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib25 "Netconfeval: can LLMs facilitate network configuration?"), [40](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib24 "Telecomgpt: a framework to build telecom-specific large language models")] rather than autonomous multi-step orchestration workflows.

Existing agentic AI frameworks [[37](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib10 "ReAct: synergizing reasoning and acting in language models"), [32](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib31 "A survey on large language model based autonomous agents"), [17](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib32 "Camel: communicative agents for\" mind\" exploration of large language model society")], whilst demonstrating effectiveness in domains such as energy management and software development, have not been systematically applied to IBN orchestration. The telecommunications domain presents unique challenges including safety-critical operation requirements, strict latency constraints, necessity for continuous adaptation to dynamic network states, and regulatory demands for decision auditability. The 6G vision articulated by Saad et al. [[25](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib35 "A vision of 6G wireless systems: applications, trends, technologies, and open research problems")] and Wang et al. [[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib1 "On the road to 6g: visions, requirements, key technologies, and testbeds")] emphasises autonomous network management and AI-driven orchestration as essential requirements, yet practical implementations employing LLM-based agentic reasoning remain largely unexplored. This work addresses this gap by presenting a hierarchical multi-agent architecture specifically designed for IBN in 6G environments, where domain-specific specialist agents collaboratively translate natural language operational intents into executable network slice configurations through iterative reasoning protocols.

## III System Model and Problem Formulation

This section formalises the IBN orchestration problem for 6G environments. We begin by characterising the standardised use case categories that define Quality of Service (QoS) requirements, then establish the formal network system model, introduce the evaluation framework, and finally present the mathematical problem formulation.

### III-A 6G Use Case Categories and QoS Requirements

The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) has defined foundational service categories for next-generation wireless systems that establish distinct QoS profiles. Whilst the ITU has expanded 5G’s three usage scenarios namely Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC), enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB), and massive Machine-Type Communication (mMTC), into six distinct 6G categories [[18](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib12 "The ITU vision and framework for 6G: scenarios, capabilities, and enablers")], we adopt the foundational three-category framework that underpins contemporary 6G service classification. This choice reflects two considerations: first, the fundamental QoS requirements that distinguish latency-critical, capacity-critical, and connectivity-critical applications remain architecturally relevant in 6G systems; second, the URLLC, eMBB, mMTC terminology maintains widespread recognition in telecommunications research and operational practice, facilitating clear communication of evaluation scope. This framework remains architecturally relevant as the fundamental QoS requirements that distinguish latency-critical, capacity-critical, and connectivity-critical applications persist across the evolved taxonomy.

Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC) encompasses safety-critical and time-sensitive applications requiring end-to-end latency below 10 milliseconds and reliability exceeding 99.999 percent [[9](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib36 "Toward massive, ultrareliable, and low-latency wireless communication with short packets"), [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib37 "Study on scenarios and requirements for next generation access technologies")]. Representative use cases include industrial automation systems with closed-loop control, autonomous vehicle coordination, remote surgical procedures, and real-time gaming for competitive e-sports [[4](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib38 "Ultrareliable and low-latency wireless communication: tail, risk, and scale")]. URLLC applications mandate edge-tier compute placement to minimise propagation delay and require guaranteed resource allocation to prevent packet loss during congestion.

Enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB) addresses high-capacity applications requiring substantial throughput (multi-gigabit per second per user) whilst tolerating moderate latency (20 to 50 milliseconds) [[28](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib39 "5G: a tutorial overview of standards, trials, challenges, deployment, and practice")]. Characteristic use cases include ultra-high-definition video streaming (4K/8K resolution), immersive extended reality experiences, fixed wireless access for residential broadband, and high-density public Wi-Fi deployments [[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib1 "On the road to 6g: visions, requirements, key technologies, and testbeds")]. eMBB applications can leverage regional-tier data centres where network buffering masks propagation latency, prioritising spectrum efficiency and aggregate capacity over minimal delay.

Massive Machine-Type Communication (mMTC) supports extremely high device densities with low per-device data rates and high latency tolerance (seconds to minutes) [[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib37 "Study on scenarios and requirements for next generation access technologies")]. Typical deployments include smart metering infrastructure, agricultural sensor networks, environmental monitoring systems, and industrial IoT telemetry. mMTC applications favour wide-area coverage and energy-efficient transmission, often utilising low-band spectrum for superior propagation characteristics and extended battery life.

These categorical distinctions inform the design of our evaluation framework (Section [III-C](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#S3.SS3 "III-C Evaluation Framework ‣ III System Model and Problem Formulation ‣ Agentic AI Empowered Intent-Based Networking for 6G")) and enable context-dependent weighting of quality metrics. The hierarchical agentic system need to recognise implicit QoS requirements from natural language intent expressions and map them to appropriate technical configurations aligned with these standardised service profiles.

### III-B Network System Model

### III-C Evaluation Framework

Evaluating agentic systems in qualitative domains such as IBN presents methodological challenges due to the absence of a single objectively correct configuration for a given intent. To address this, we introduce a hybrid evaluation framework consisting of two complementary metrics: Semantic Accuracy (i.e., qualitative alignment with expert expectations) and Engineering Utility (i.e., quantitative assessment of technical optimality).

#### III-C1 Semantic Accuracy, i.e., Golden Standard

We define a benchmark set of representative test scenarios spanning diverse 6G use cases. Each scenario consists of a natural language intent and a corresponding expert-defined ideal configuration. This ideal configuration, termed the “Golden Standard”, is specified by human domain experts with knowledge of RAN resource allocation and core network topology.

System performance is evaluated by comparing the agent-generated configuration 𝒜∗\mathcal{A}^{\*} against the Golden Standard. We define a three-level semantic accuracy score:

|  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | A​(𝒜∗,𝒜golden)={1.0if ​𝒜∗=𝒜golden0.5if ​𝒜∗≈𝒜golden0.0otherwiseA(\mathcal{A}^{\*},\mathcal{A}\_{\text{golden}})=\begin{cases}1.0&\text{if }\mathcal{A}^{\*}=\mathcal{A}\_{\text{golden}}\\ 0.5&\text{if }\mathcal{A}^{\*}\approx\mathcal{A}\_{\text{golden}}\\ 0.0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases} |  | (2) |

A Perfect Match (1.0) indicates that the system’s output exactly matches the expert configuration across all three dimensions: sector, spectrum, and UPF node. A Partial Match (0.5) is assigned when the configuration matches on two of three dimensions. No Match (0.0) indicates fundamental misalignment with expert expectations.

#### III-C3 Hybrid Evaluation Protocol

For each test scenario, we execute the system and record both the Semantic Accuracy score AA and the Engineering Utility score UU. A successful deployment satisfies two conditions: semantic alignment with expert expectations (A≥0.5A\geq 0.5) and engineering feasibility (U≥0.7U\geq 0.7). This dual-metric approach enables us to verify that the agent not only understood the operator’s intent but also implemented it optimally given the prevailing network conditions.

### III-D Problem Formulation

Given the network system model discussed in Section [III-B](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#S3.SS2 "III-B Network System Model ‣ III System Model and Problem Formulation ‣ Agentic AI Empowered Intent-Based Networking for 6G") and evaluation framework discussed in Section [III-C](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#S3.SS3 "III-C Evaluation Framework ‣ III System Model and Problem Formulation ‣ Agentic AI Empowered Intent-Based Networking for 6G"), we now formally state the IBN orchestration problem. The system seeks an optimal configuration that maximises the utility function whilst satisfying operational constraints:

|  |  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | 𝒜∗=arg⁡max𝒜⁡U​(𝒜,I,𝒮)\mathcal{A}^{\*}=\arg\max\_{\mathcal{A}}\,U(\mathcal{A},I,\mathcal{S}) | |  | (7a) |
|  | | | | |
|  | s.t.Lr∗\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad L\_{r^{\*}} | ≤Lmax\displaystyle\leq L\_{\text{max}} |  | (7b) |
|  | b∗\displaystyle b^{\*} | ∈Br∗\displaystyle\in B\_{r^{\*}} |  | (7c) |
|  | κc∗\displaystyle\kappa\_{c^{\*}} | ≤κmax\displaystyle\leq\kappa\_{\text{max}} |  | (7d) |
|  | τ​(r∗,c∗)\displaystyle\tau(r^{\*},c^{\*}) | ≤τreq​(I)\displaystyle\leq\tau\_{\text{req}}(I) |  | (7e) |

where U​(𝒜,I,𝒮)U(\mathcal{A},I,\mathcal{S}) is the utility function defined in Equation ([3](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#S3.E3 "In III-C2 Engineering Utility Function ‣ III-C Evaluation Framework ‣ III System Model and Problem Formulation ‣ Agentic AI Empowered Intent-Based Networking for 6G")), LmaxL\_{\text{max}} is the sector’s load threshold, Br∗B\_{r^{\*}} denotes available spectrum bands, κmax\kappa\_{\text{max}} is the computation capacity of the UPF node, and τreq​(I)\tau\_{\text{req}}(I) denotes the latency threshold extracted from the operator’s intent.

Unlike classical optimisation problems where objectives are expressed as mathematical functions, the intent II in IBN systems is unstructured natural language (e.g., “Configure network for a sports tournament requiring ultra-low latency and high user density”). This necessitates a semantic reasoning layer capable of mapping linguistic abstractions (such as “ultra-low latency” or “high density”) to concrete technical constraints and subsequently resolving them against the current network state 𝒮\mathcal{S}.

The challenge lies not in solving a constrained optimisation problem with known parameters, but rather in the interpretation and extraction of those parameters from natural language. Traditional mathematical optimisation techniques assume that decision variables, objective functions, and constraints are explicitly specified. In contrast, IBN requires semantic understanding to identify which constraints are relevant (e.g., latency versus capacity), extract implicit numerical thresholds (e.g., mapping “ultra-low latency” to τ<10\tau<10 ms), and infer use case categories that determine utility function weights.

This semantic gap motivates the agentic AI architecture presented in Section [IV](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#S4 "IV Agentic AI Architecture and Implementation ‣ Agentic AI Empowered Intent-Based Networking for 6G"), in which LLMs serve as the reasoning layer that bridges natural language intent and formal technical specifications.

## IV Agentic AI Architecture and Implementation

This section details the hierarchical multi-agent architecture and its practical implementation. We describe the agent organisation, coordination protocol, prompt engineering strategy, network state representation, and iterative reasoning mechanism that collectively enable autonomous intent translation.

### IV-A Hierarchical Multi-Agent Architecture

To address the semantic reasoning and constraint satisfaction challenges identified in Section [III](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#S3 "III System Model and Problem Formulation ‣ Agentic AI Empowered Intent-Based Networking for 6G"), we propose a hierarchical agentic AI architecture inspired by the ReAct (Reason+Act) paradigm [[37](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib10 "ReAct: synergizing reasoning and acting in language models")]. The system comprises three LLM-based agents organised in a two-tier structure: a central orchestrator agent and two domain-specific specialist agents, as shown in Fig. [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ IV-A Hierarchical Multi-Agent Architecture ‣ IV Agentic AI Architecture and Implementation ‣ Agentic AI Empowered Intent-Based Networking for 6G").

![Refer to caption](fig/1.jpg)


Figure 2: Hierarchical multi-agent architecture for IBN orchestration.

#### IV-A1 Orchestrator Agent

The orchestrator serves as the central decision-making entity and the unified interface for human operators. Its primary responsibilities include intent parsing (i.e., decomposing the high-level intent II into domain-specific sub-problems), delegation to appropriate specialists, synthesis of recommendations, and execution of final configurations. The orchestrator operates through iterative Thought-Action-Observation cycles, generating reasoning traces, executing tool calls, and refining its plan based on feedback.

#### IV-A2 Specialist Agents

To mitigate context window limitations and reasoning bottlenecks inherent in monolithic LLM systems, we offload domain-specific reasoning to specialised agents. These agents do not possess direct tool access; instead, they function as constrained reasoning engines that receive environmental context from the orchestrator.

The RAN Specialist Agent embodies expertise in radio resource management, with policies for spectrum allocation based on user density and propagation requirements. The agent receives a structured representation of the relevant RAN sector’s state (including current load, active user count, and available spectrum) and returns a justified recommendation in natural language.

The Core Specialist Agent specialises in network topology and UPF placement, with guidelines for latency optimisation and compute resource allocation. Upon receiving a query from the orchestrator, the Core specialist evaluates candidate UPF nodes against latency and capacity constraints, returning a placement recommendation with justification.

This modular design facilitates independent refinement of specialist knowledge without necessitating retraining of the central orchestrator.

### IV-B Sequential Coordination Protocol

As shown in Fig. [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ IV-B Sequential Coordination Protocol ‣ IV Agentic AI Architecture and Implementation ‣ Agentic AI Empowered Intent-Based Networking for 6G"), the system employs a sequential consultation protocol to resolve multi-domain constraints systematically. During initialisation, the orchestrator receives intent II from the human operator and parses it to identify key requirements (e.g., latency thresholds, user density, reliability constraints). The orchestrator then consults the RAN specialist to establish physical layer feasibility. The query specifies the target sector extracted from the intent and asks whether the sector can accommodate the implied traffic load and QoS requirements. The RAN specialist examines sector load LrL\_{r} and available spectrum BrB\_{r}, returning a spectrum band recommendation b∗b^{\*} or a warning if the sector is congested.

![Refer to caption](fig/2.png)


Figure 3: Sequential coordination protocol for multi-domain network slice provisioning.

Conditioned on the RAN recommendation, the orchestrator proceeds to consult the Core specialist. The query incorporates the RAN decision to ensure compatibility. The Core specialist evaluates candidate nodes based on their latency profiles to the target sector from the latency matrix 𝐓\mathbf{T} and compute availability κc\kappa\_{c}, selecting the optimal UPF node c∗c^{\*}. The orchestrator then aggregates the recommendations (r∗,b∗,c∗)(r^{\*},b^{\*},c^{\*}) and performs a final consistency check. This includes verifying that the selected UPF node achieves acceptable latency to the RAN sector and that no resource conflicts exist. Upon validation, the orchestrator invokes the provisioning tool, which applies the configuration to the network controller.

This decoupled, sequential approach offers three principal advantages. First, interpretability is enhanced as each agent’s reasoning trace is preserved, enabling operators to audit decision rationale. Second, modularity allows specialist agents to be independently upgraded or replaced without disrupting the orchestrator. Third, constraint propagation is achieved by conditioning Core decisions on RAN outputs, ensuring that inter-domain dependencies are respected. However, this sequential protocol assumes that RAN and Core decisions are largely independent given the intent, an assumption that may not hold for certain advanced slicing scenarios requiring joint optimisation across domains.

### IV-C Agent Prompt Engineering

The core intelligence of our system resides in the prompt engineering of the three LLM agents. We employ an agent-specific prompting strategy, in which each agent is assigned a specific expert role and a set of operational constraints encoded directly within its system prompt.

The orchestrator is initialised with a system prompt that defines its role as a “6G Intent Coordinator.” It is explicitly instructed to refrain from making technical decisions directly but instead to delegate domain-specific sub-problems to appropriate specialists. The prompt includes a structured description of available tools (call\_agent for specialist consultation and provision\_slice for configuration execution) and encodes the ReAct protocol through explicit formatting guidelines. The orchestrator’s prompt instructs it to generate structured outputs in the following format:

```
THOUGHT: [Reasoning about current state]
ACTION: [Tool invocation with parameters]
OBSERVATION: [Result from tool execution]
```

This structured generation ensures that the orchestrator’s decision-making process remains transparent and auditable. The prompt further specifies coordination rules, such as “ALWAYS consult specialists before provisioning” and “Synthesise recommendations from multiple agents before final decision,” which prevent premature configuration application.

The specialist agents (RAN and Core) are prompted with domain-specific policies that encode expert knowledge. The RAN specialist prompt includes quantitative decision rules. For instance, it specifies: “For high user density scenarios (exceeding 10k active users), prioritise millimetre-wave spectrum if available, as mid-band cannot handle the capacity.” The prompt further encodes reliability policies: “For high-reliability or wide-coverage requirements, favour mid-band spectrum due to its superior propagation characteristics compared to mmWave.” Additionally, the prompt instructs the agent to issue congestion warnings: “If a sector’s load percentage exceeds 80 percent, explicitly warn the orchestrator that the sector is approaching capacity limits.”

The Core specialist prompt encodes latency-centric policies. It specifies: “For URLLC intents requiring latency below 10 milliseconds, specify the selection of multi-access edge computing (MEC) nodes. Regional or centralised data centres cannot satisfy such stringent latency constraints.” Conversely, for standard latency requirements (exceeding 20 milliseconds), the prompt instructs the agent to recommend regional data centres to optimise cost-efficiency by avoiding over-utilisation of expensive edge resources. The prompt further includes capacity verification rules: “Before recommending a UPF node, verify that its current compute load is below 85 percent to ensure sufficient headroom for the new slice.”

Each specialist prompt concludes with an explicit output format requirement, requiring that recommendations be structured as:

```
RECOMMENDATION: [Action] at [Location]
because [Reason].
```

This enforced structure ensures that specialist outputs are consistently parsable by the orchestrator and provide rationale for audit purposes. All prompts are stored as Markdown files external to the Python codebase, enabling rapid iteration and version control of agent behaviours without modifying application logic.

### IV-D Network State Representation

The agents interact with the network through structured representations of network state 𝒮\mathcal{S}. For experimental validation, we implement a simplified network state model as a structured JSON object. This approach enables controlled evaluation of the agentic system’s reasoning capabilities without the complexity of interfacing with production network management platforms. The JSON representation serves as a mock network state that captures the essential characteristics defined in our system model whilst abstracting away implementation details of actual telecommunications infrastructure.

The structured representation contains two primary components. The sectors object maps sector identifiers to their current operational state. Each sector entry includes active\_users (the count of connected devices), load\_percentage (current utilisation as a percentage of maximum capacity), and spectrum\_available\_mhz (a nested object specifying available capacity in megahertz for each frequency band: mmWave, mid\_band, and low\_band). The nodes object similarly maps node identifiers to their operational characteristics. Each node entry includes type (classification as edge, regional, or core), latency\_to\_ran\_ms (end-to-end latency from the RAN edge in milliseconds), and compute\_load\_percent (current computational utilisation).

During execution, when a specialist agent is invoked by the orchestrator agent, the system performs context injection. The function first loads the agent’s system prompt from its corresponding Markdown file. It then reads the network state representation and serialises it as a JSON string. This serialised state is appended to the user message sent to the agent, formatted as:

```
ORCHESTRATOR REQUEST:
[Query from Orchestrator]

CURRENT NETWORK STATE:
[JSON representation of network state]

Provide your expert recommendation based
on this state.
```

This injection mechanism ensures that the specialist’s reasoning is grounded in verified network conditions. The LLM receives both the task description and the complete environmental state within a single context window, enabling it to generate recommendations that respect physical constraints without requiring iterative tool calls. This context grounding prevents hallucinations regarding resource availability, which is a known failure mode in unconstrained LLM generation [[13](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#bib.bib11 "Survey of hallucination in natural language generation")].

After each LLM call, the orchestrator’s response is appended to the conversation history. The response is then parsed to detect action strings through pattern matching. If the response contains ACTION: CALL\_AGENT, the system extracts the agent name and request, invokes functions to consult specialist agents, and appends the specialist’s response as an observation to the conversation history. If the response contains ACTION: PROVISION\_SLICE, the system extracts the configuration parameters and invokes functions to perform slice provisioning. If the response contains ACTION: FINISH, the loop terminates.

All LLM calls are made with temperature set to 0.0 to ensure deterministic reasoning and reproducibility across experimental runs. We use the Cerebras Cloud API with the Llama-3.3-70B model to obtain agents’ responses. Token usage for each LLM call (both orchestrator and specialist invocations) is tracked and aggregated for computational efficiency analysis.

### V-B Performance on Benchmark Scenarios

Table [III](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06640v1#S5.T3 "TABLE III ‣ V-A Experimental Setup ‣ V Experiments ‣ Agentic AI Empowered Intent-Based Networking for 6G") presents system performance across the 12 benchmark scenarios. The proposed multi-agent architecture achieves a mean Semantic Accuracy of 0.667 (standard deviation 0.246), with four scenarios (33%) receiving Perfect Match scores of 1.0 and seven scenarios (58%) achieving Partial Match scores of 0.5. The mean Engineering Utility score of 0.747 (standard deviation 0.169) exceeds the predefined feasibility threshold of 0.70, demonstrating that provisioned configurations satisfy technical constraints.

Computational efficiency metrics reveal an average token consumption of 13,281 tokens per provisioning decision. The mean decision latency of 3.8 seconds falls within acceptable bounds for non-real-time provisioning workflows. The system consistently converges within 3.0 ReAct iterations across all scenarios, demonstrating efficient reasoning without excessive computational overhead.

We observed a systematic bias from these results. Analysis of the benchmark results revealed a systematic pattern in decision-making: scenarios requiring regional data centre placement for delay-tolerant traffic exhibited lower semantic accuracy compared to ultra-low latency scenarios. Manual inspection of Core specialist reasoning traces identified the root cause as insufficiently precise policy encoding in the agent’s system prompt. The Core specialist consistently selected the lowest-latency option available regardless of traffic type, failing to apply cost-efficiency policies for delay-tolerant applications such as eMBB streaming and mMTC sensor deployments.

The issue stemmed from weak directive language in the Core specialist prompt. The original instruction stated that regional data centres were “acceptable” for moderate latency requirements, which the language model interpreted as “only use if nothing better exists” rather than “prefer for cost efficiency when latency tolerance permits.” The prompt lacked explicit traffic-type awareness, failing to distinguish between URLLC applications where milliseconds matter for safety and eMBB or mMTC applications where regional placement is operationally equivalent but economically superior.

We revised the Core specialist prompt to encode traffic-aware placement policies more explicitly. The updated prompt instructs the agent to: (1) reserve edge nodes exclusively for URLLC traffic with safety-critical latency requirements, (2) prefer regional data centres for eMBB streaming applications where 30–50 ms latency is imperceptible to end users due to client-side buffering, and (3) mandate regional placement for mMTC deployments exhibiting high latency tolerance. The revised prompt replaces passive language (“acceptable”) with directive language (“prefer”, “avoid”, “mandate”) and includes worked examples demonstrating correct regional data centre selection for delay-tolerant traffic.

Following prompt revision, system performance improved modestly from 0.667 to 0.708 semantic accuracy (6 to 7 scenarios achieving perfect match). Whilst this improvement is statistically modest, it provides empirical evidence for two critical observations regarding prompt engineering in agentic systems. Seemingly minor linguistic variations in system prompts can induce measurable behavioural differences that compound across deployment scenarios. The shift from “acceptable” to “prefer” altered decision logic in multiple scenarios, demonstrating that language models interpret subtle lexical cues with consequential downstream effects.

This finding exposes a critical challenge for production deployment of agentic systems: how detailed should system prompts be to achieve reliable behaviour without incurring prohibitive computational costs or creating brittle over-specification? Overly concise prompts risk ambiguity and inconsistent interpretation, as demonstrated by the original “acceptable” formulation. Conversely, exhaustively detailed prompts increase token consumption (and thus inference cost), potentially introduce contradictory instructions, and may not generalise to more complex network topologies. The optimal prompt design strategy likely varies across domains, model capabilities, and deployment contexts, representing an open research question requiring systematic investigation.

## VI Discussions

The experimental results provide several insights into agentic AI system design for network automation. First, the identification and correction of the Core specialist’s “latency greedy” behaviour demonstrates that LLM-based systems can exhibit subtle prompt-induced biases that manifest as systematic failure patterns rather than random errors. Unlike traditional software bugs that cause immediate crashes, prompt engineering issues create behavioural tendencies that compound across scenarios, requiring careful error pattern analysis to diagnose. The 25 percentage point projected improvement from prompt revision (0.667 to 0.917 accuracy) validates prompt engineering as a critical architectural component requiring rigorous validation.

Second, the comparative analysis reveals fundamental trade-offs between decision quality and computational efficiency. The multi-agent system achieves superior semantic understanding (+8% over monolithic) but requires 7×\times more tokens and 5×\times longer execution time. For production deployments, this suggests a tiered architecture: complex, ambiguous intents processed by the full multi-agent system; straightforward intents handled by lightweight monolithic agents; and repetitive patterns addressed by cached rule-based configurations. Such hybrid systems could optimise the accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier.

Third, the ablation studies indicate that ReAct iterative refinement provides the largest marginal contribution, followed by engineered system prompts and specialist decomposition. This ordering suggests that architectural efforts should prioritise robust reasoning protocols and precise prompt engineering over organisational structure. However, these findings are specific to the tested scenario characteristics (largely decoupled RAN/Core decisions, moderate complexity intents) and may not generalise to more sophisticated IBN workflows requiring tighter cross-domain coordination.

The token efficiency comparison (proposed 13,281 versus monolithic 1,894) highlights a critical challenge for LLM-based network automation: inference costs scale with architectural complexity. At current Cerebras pricing (1 USD per million tokens), the proposed system costs approximately 0.013 USD per provisioning decision. Future work should investigate prompt compression techniques, knowledge distillation to smaller models, and caching strategies to reduce per-decision token consumption whilst preserving reasoning quality.

Regarding the simplified network representation used in this study, we note that whilst the experimental evaluation employs mock network data for controlled validation, the architectural principles and reasoning patterns are designed to generalise to production environments. Future work should investigate integration with actual network management platforms and evaluate performance using real-time telemetry data. Specifically, O-RAN environments represent a promising deployment target, where the orchestrator agent could function as a Non-Real-Time RAN Intelligent Controller (Non-RT RIC) application coordinating xApps and rApps configurations based on natural language operator intents, interfacing with E2 telemetry interfaces for network state acquisition. The concept of representing network state as structured data accessible to agents is more fundamental than the specific implementation approach, and similar representations could be derived from network management systems, operational support systems, or O-RAN RIC frameworks in production deployments.

## VII Conclusion

This paper presented a hierarchical multi-agent framework for IBN in 6G environments, demonstrating that LLM-powered agentic systems can autonomously translate natural language operational intents into executable network slice configurations. The architecture employs an orchestrator agent coordinating two domain-specific specialist agents through iterative ReAct reasoning, with all agents grounded in structured network state representations. Experimental evaluation across diverse benchmark scenarios showed that the proposed system outperforms monolithic agents, rule-based systems, and direct LLM prompting, with ablation studies confirming that iterative refinement and prompt engineering provide the most significant architectural contributions. As 6G networks and O-RAN architectures evolve towards autonomous operation, agentic AI frameworks offer a promising path forward for transforming natural language intents into network-wide configurations whilst maintaining transparency and operational trust through interpretable reasoning traces.
